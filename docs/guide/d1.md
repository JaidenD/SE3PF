## What this layer does

This layer turns the Franka Panda into a `System` that the generic `Controller<System>` can drive, and it supplies the seventh output β that makes the arm's A(q) square. Everything else in the library (metric, path, tube, controller) is shared with the rigid body; apart from the controller's n = 7 branch, only these files know that the plant is a seven-joint arm.

### The problem the layer solves

The Panda has n = 7 joints, but its output Π(q), the flange pose, lives in the 6-dimensional SE(3). So J_b(q) is a 6 × 7 matrix (`Mat67`), and the arm has r = n − 6 = 1 redundant degree of freedom. The six tubular outputs ȳ = (η, ξ) = Ψ⁻¹(Π(q)) fix only six of the seven velocity directions.

Differentiating Π(q) = Ψ(η, ξ) gives J_b q̇ = J_Ψ (η̇, ξ̇), so (η̇, ξ̇) = J_Ψ⁻¹ J_b q̇. That 6 × 7 map has a one-dimensional kernel, ker J_b(q): the self-motion, where the joints move and the flange does not. Feedback linearization needs ȳ̇ = A(q) q̇ with A square and invertible, so a seventh output β must observe exactly that missing direction.

The paper's answer has three pieces, and each is one class in `lift.hpp`:

1. The lift q*(s) (Assumption 1): a smooth closed curve of configurations with Π(q*(s)) = γ(s) and J_b(q*(s)) of full rank. The code picks, at each s, the configuration on the self-motion manifold over γ(s) nearest the rest posture q_r. Class `Lift`.
2. The kernel frame X(s) (Lemma 3): a unit vector spanning ker J_b(q*(s)), smooth and periodic in s. For r = 1 it is the normalized generalized cross product of the rows of J_b. Class `KernelFrame`, built on the free function `kernelVector`.
3. The redundancy output β(q) = X(η)ᵀ(q − q*(η)) (eq:beta-flat), the signed distance from the lift along the self-motion direction, with its differential dβ (eq:dbeta). Class `LiftRedundancy`.

### Why β makes A square and invertible

With β the output vector is ȳ = (η, ξ, β) ∈ R⁷ and A(q) = [J_Ψ⁻¹ J_b; dβ] is 7 × 7 (eq:A). Inside the tube J_Ψ is invertible, so the first six rows have rank 6 and their kernel is ker J_b(q). A is therefore invertible exactly when dβ does not vanish on ker J_b(q).

On the lift, ker J_b(q*(η)) is spanned by X(η), and the flange does not move along X, so dη X = 0. Then eq:dbeta gives dβ X = Xᵀ X = 1, which is not zero, so A is invertible on the lift. By continuity it stays invertible near the lift (Proposition 2).

The measured margin: σ_min(A) = 0.0474 at the paper's start, the smallest value along the whole 20 s run, and 0.0551 at the self-motion start. The controller refuses a step when σ_min(A) < 1e-9 (`include/se3pf/controller.hpp:305`).

### Offline and online parts

Everything expensive happens once, before the first control cycle. The online part only reads splines and evaluates the forward kinematics.

| Stage | What runs | Cost (Release) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offline | `Lift`: 2001 projected-Newton solves along the path, then a cubic Hermite spline of q* and q*′ | about 0.02 s |
| Offline | `KernelFrame`: 2001 kernel vectors (7 determinants of 6 × 6 each), then a spline of X and X′ | about 0.003 s |
| Offline | `startConfiguration` twice: Newton on the 7 × 7 system [J_b; pᵀ] | 5 and 4 updates |
| Online, 1 or 3 times per step | `PandaArm::output(q, &Jb)`: flange pose and body Jacobian | about 0.3 µs per call |
| Online, 1 or 3 times per step | `LiftRedundancy::evaluate`: two spline lookups and two dot products | about 0.04 µs per call |
| Online, whole step | `Controller<PandaArm>::step` | 12–17 µs (paper: 27 µs) |

The whole `PandaExample`, the tube included, is built in about 0.03 s. The timings were measured on a heavily loaded 12-core machine (load average 14–20), where the step took 15–17 µs; the README reports about 12 µs. Treat all timings as orders of magnitude.

While the arm moves, the controller evaluates the outputs three times per step: at q and at the two points q ± h q̇ (h = 1e-5) of the central difference for Ȧq̇. At rest (q̇ = 0, the paper run's first step) Ȧq̇ = 0 and the two extra evaluations are skipped (`include/se3pf/controller.hpp:263`).

### The example in one paragraph

`PandaExample` builds the paper's setup: W with ℓ = 0.1 m, a coning circle of radius 0.12 m around (0.40, 0, 0.45) m with cone angle 0.25 rad (L = 0.77008765), its tube, the rest-posture lift, the kernel frame and β. `simulate` then runs the closed loop at the joint-acceleration level, where eq:fb with an exact model makes q̈ = q̈*, so no dynamics are needed. Two runs exist: the paper's (start at rest at Ψ(0.1, ξ₀) with β = 0, 20 s) and a self-motion run (start on the path with β = 0.2, one loop) that exercises the β channel.
